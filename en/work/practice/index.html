02 / Project management tool

Practice

Delivery progress, team effort and project profit in one view, so a practice lead knows which projects need attention before the weekly meeting.

Project type
Self-initiated product design and interactive prototype
Users
Directors of small architecture practices, project teams and clients
My role
Product concept, workflow mapping, information architecture, interface design and prototyping
Status
The interactive web demo is live; there is also an iOS demo project, not yet tested on a real device.

01 | Why build this

On an architecture project, drawings moving to plan doesn’t mean the project is doing well as a business. A project may already be in the construction-documentation stage yet have used more hours than planned because consultant information keeps needing coordination. A design fee may have been invoiced but not yet paid.

More than ten years of architectural design and project work showed me that this information usually lives in separate places: the programme in a schedule, consultant deliverables in emails and meeting minutes, staff time in timesheets, and fees and payments in yet another spreadsheet. To decide whether a project needs stepping in, a director often has to piece all of this together first.

So I defined Practice’s core question as: when a practice runs several projects at once, how can the director quickly tell which project needs attention, why, and what to look at next?

02 | Designing for what each role needs to do

I started from the tasks of three kinds of users on the same architecture project, and used them to decide what each screen shows first.

Role Questions they need answered The design
Practice director Which project is off track? Do I need to follow up on cost, consultant deliverables or payments? Project overview, timeline, stage budgets, forecast profit, cash alerts and timesheet approval.
Project team Which projects am I on? Which stage did my time go to this week? Assigned projects, a weekly timesheet filled in by project and stage, and submission and review status.
Client Where is the project up to? What documents can I see? When is the next fee due? Project progress, published documents, invoices and payment notices. The demo includes a sample plan and massing.

For example, on the same house project, the director can see labour spent in the construction-documentation stage and the forecast remaining cost, staff log their hours for that stage, and the client sees the current progress and the documents the director has published. All three views are organised around the same project; the priority of information comes from the task each person needs to complete.

03 | Letting the overview answer “which project should I look at?”

I put delivery progress, payment received, cost and forecast profit side by side on every project card. All cards use the same order, so the director can compare projects quickly. Rings show the proportions, while the contract amount, payments received and forecast profit appear as actual figures. That way the director can spot an anomaly first, and also see how much money it involves.

I also kept key information where the comparison happens. Cost alerts appear directly on the relevant project card, and the overview can be sorted by risk. Having found a problem, the director can go on to the project details, stage budget or timesheets, moving from “this project needs watching” to “this is what I need to check”.

Take the demo project Harbour Retail: delivery progress is 45% and 54% of the design fee has been received, but staff cost has reached 51% of the contract, above the 50% threshold that was set. The same card also shows an overdue invoice. The director can follow up two separate questions: why the current stage has used so much labour, and which payment needs chasing.

04 | Connecting staff timesheets to the director’s view of cost

Timesheets are filled in week by week. Staff record time by project and design stage, save a draft, then submit it for review. The director sees staff cost on the project card and can drill into the time records. Time that used to be logged only for the record becomes something that helps the practice judge whether effort on a project is on track.

Cash alerts let you set a threshold and choose whether it is calculated against the contract amount or the cost budget. The first answers “how much of the fee has labour used up?”; the second answers “how much of the planned cost has labour consumed?”. I wrote the basis and the actual percentage into the alert text, so the director knows why it was triggered and can explain it in the meeting.

For profit, I separated cost to date, forecast remaining cost and forecast direct profit. The director sees a forecast of the result at completion based on current estimates, which can be updated as the project moves on. For projects still in design, the remaining work directly affects that forecast.

05 | Giving clients a clear window onto delivery

The client portal is organised around three practical questions: how is the project going, what documents can I see, and what fees are outstanding. The director controls which projects and document categories each client can access, then decides which progress updates, drawings, models and invoices are visible.

Payment works through invoices and bank-transfer reconciliation. After seeing the amount and due date, the client can submit a transfer reference. The system shows it as awaiting reconciliation until the director confirms the money has arrived, then updates the payment status. I made “client has reported payment” and “practice has confirmed receipt” two separate states, so both sides know who needs to act next.

06 | Testing the prototype, and what’s next

I built Practice as an interactive demo that needs no login. Visitors can switch between director, staff and client views, look at project charts, try filling in a timesheet, adjust alert thresholds and try reporting a payment. The interface works in Chinese and English, in light and dark. The demo uses made-up data and resets when you reload; the drawings and models are illustrative samples.

So far, testing has focused on whether the key flows work end to end. Next, I’ll ask directors of small practices to complete one concrete task: without opening any project details, find the project that most needs attention and explain how you decided. I’ll watch which metric they look at first, whether they understand the basis of the alerts, and whether, once they open the details, they can find the matching timesheet or budget information. The results will shape the order of information on the cards, the chart labels and the alert wording.
