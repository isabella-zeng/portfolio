01 / iOS community product

ORA Pixel Café

An iOS community design about who's here right now, and who's up for a chat.

Project type
iOS community product, interactive prototype
Audience
People who study or work remotely from cafés
Design scope
Information architecture, core interactions, pixel visual system, iOS prototype and game integration
Built with
SwiftUI, SwiftData, Godot

Working alone in a café, I’ve imagined this moment: the person at the next table is also sketching designs, and I’d love to ask what they’re working on, but I’m worried about interrupting. ORA starts from that hesitation. It explores how a community product can help people find company, while leaving room for those who just want to work quietly.

Starting from one concrete social decision

Before saying hello to a stranger, I’d want to know three things. Are they in the same café? Do we have something in common to talk about? Is now a good moment to reach out?

Those three questions set the order of information in ORA. The café and who’s present help people find who they might run into; profession, skills and posts give them something to talk about; and the chat button comes only after you’ve learned about the other person. You can follow that path and reach out, or simply see what everyone is up to. Either choice ends the visit naturally.

Why the home screen shows who’s here first

When you open ORA, the first thing under the greeting is a row of member avatars, and only then the café filter and community feed. Space on the first screen of a phone is limited, so putting avatars here pushes the first post down a little. I’m willing to make that trade, because for someone who has just sat down, “who else is here today” is closer to their situation than “who posted what recently”.

The feed still matters: it gives the avatars context. Seeing Alice’s avatar only tells you who she is; reading that she has just finished drawing an onboarding flow gives you something specific to talk about. You can also filter by café to bring your attention back to the place you care about.

Giving a hello a reason

The members page lets you search by name, profession or skill, and filter by online status. That way you can find people you know, or start from a shared interest. From a member’s profile you can tap “Say hi” to start a conversation.

I put this step on the profile so that people have a chance to learn about each other before sending a message. The button label was chosen deliberately as the everyday “Say hi”. “Connect” sounds like you’re about to maintain a relationship, while a hello only asks you to decide what you want to say right now.

Events as another way into the community

A private message means first deciding who to talk to and what to say. A themed event gives people a ready-made topic. So ORA keeps two ways to take part: if you’re interested in a person, look at their profile; if you’re interested in something happening, sign up for an event.

The events page is organised by date, with the time, spots and sign-up action together. The segmented pixel bar on each card borrows a progress display common in games: one glance tells you whether an event is nearly full, and the count confirms the exact number of places. After signing up you can find the event again under “My events”, or cancel. The design covers both the moment before deciding and the moment after changing your mind.

Why pixel art

ORA includes a pixel-art office you can enter and watch. If only the game were pixel art, people moving from the community into the game would have to get to know everyone again. So I started from the characters and used the eight in-game character portraits as community avatars. You meet Alice in the feed first, and when you enter the office you recognise her familiar face. That link matters more than simply matching colours.

The visual system follows the same idea. The cream background and dark coffee outlines echo the café; pixel headings, stepped corners and segmented progress bars echo the game. When pressed, buttons move into their hard shadow at the bottom right, giving a slight sinking feedback. Even in the most ordinary tap, you can feel the interface’s personality.

From the community into “a little world in a box”

On the game page, tap “Enter the little world” and the phone turns to landscape to show the whole office. Leaving it brings you back to the portrait community. The game has its own entry point, and browsing the feed, finding members and signing up for events each have their own complete paths.

This switch was also a problem that only got solved once the design reached a device. The first time I embedded the game on the phone, the office showed up in one corner of the screen. Checking in the simulator showed the game was still using the fixed window size from desktop. Only after adjusting the mobile display logic did the scene fill the landscape screen. That detail reminded me that an experience spanning two interfaces includes every step of rotating, waiting, leaving and coming back.

The prototype and what to test next

The final prototype includes first-time onboarding and five tabs: feed, members, events, chat and game. The community side uses local sample data, so you can try posting, viewing members, signing up for events and opening a chat. The embedded game is based on the open-source project Microverse; my work on this project focused on the community product design, the iOS implementation, and the visual and interaction link between the game and the community.

Next, the thing I most want to test is the idea of “being here”. When people see avatars and statuses, can they tell apart “online”, “at this café right now” and “happy to chat”? I’ll ask testers to complete one concrete task: find someone you’d be willing to contact, and say why you think now is a good time to say hi. Whether they rely on status, location or a post that resonates will directly shape which information the next version of the home screen groups together.
